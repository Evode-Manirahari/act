# Founder Principles — Startup School 2026, applied to Actober

Distilled from talks by Jensen Huang (NVIDIA), Sam Altman (OpenAI), Blake
Scholl (Boom), Alexandr Wang (Meta Superintelligence), Boris Cherny (Claude
Code), and Jeff Dean (Google). Not a quote collection — each principle is
translated into what it means for *this* company. When a product or
engineering decision is contested, check it against this list.

## 1. Confront reality, then buy the textbook (Jensen)

NVIDIA's founding technology was wrong. They said so out loud, went to Fry's,
bought three OpenGL textbooks, and rebuilt. Boom's first algorithm was wrong
too; Scholl told Sega the truth and still asked for the money — honesty kept
the company alive.

**For Actober:** when pilot data says something isn't working (5 cards
published against a 50-card target says the review/publish loop is the
bottleneck, not capture), say it plainly and fix that — don't polish the part
that already works. Be Sega-honest with the pilot client about what works and
what doesn't; the trust is worth more than the demo.

## 2. Make something people want — big things look like toys (Sam)

ChatGPT did nothing "productive" at launch. The vision was clear (AGI); the
first steps were unclear for years, and that was fine. What's not fine is
never collecting new data points.

**For Actober:** one tech reaching a correct next step faster on one real job
is worth more than any impressive demo. The vision ("intelligence layer for
physical work, coordinating humans, agents, and robots") can be huge while
the next step stays embarrassingly small. Don't confuse the two directions.

## 3. Conviction before consensus — with evidence discipline (Alexandr)

Scale's data business was "unsexy" for years; investors passed, then wrote
think pieces. Develop an internal compass and hold it against the noise. But
the compass is built from real observation, not wishful thinking.

**For Actober:** "tacit field knowledge is capturable and transferable" is
our contrarian bet. Bill's interview validated the environment, not the
product. Every product claim stays labeled a hypothesis until a technician's
behavior confirms it. Conviction about the destination, ruthless empiricism
about the route.

## 4. Iterate at the product level — multiple shots on goal (Blake)

Boom's biggest stated regret: XB-1 was too big a prototype. The lesson is to
pick slices small enough that you can be wrong about *requirements*, not just
engineering, and still get more shots.

**For Actober:** never bet a quarter on one big build. Every slice must be
small enough that if techs ignore it, we learn why and re-aim within weeks.
The 60-day pilot is the shot clock.

## 5. Be empirical with AI — verification is the whole game (Boris)

Claude Code deletes 80% of its system prompt every model generation and adds
back only what the model demonstrably needs. The single most important thing
people get wrong with AI: not giving it a way to verify its work.

**For Actober:** every AI output must be evidence-linked (cite the card, the
moment, the footage), uncertainty-aware (honest refusal beats confident
invention — the honest empty state is a feature, keep it), correctable (a
tech can flag a wrong answer), and escalation-capable (worst case is always
"stop, call the senior tech" — never an invented procedure). Re-test
assumptions each model generation; delete scaffolding that new models no
longer need.

## 6. Your moat is data the general models will never have (Jeff)

Jeff Dean's advice on where small teams beat labs: products with access to
data the general model doesn't have, in domains where the frontier model
succeeds 0–1% of the time, not 20%. And: napkin math before building.

**For Actober:** the moat is not the chat UI or the pipeline — those are
replicable. It is the corpus of *this company's* reviewed, footage-backed,
jurisdiction-tagged diagnostic judgment, which no frontier lab can train on.
Every feature should either grow that corpus or prove its value in the
field. Napkin math worth keeping: one avoided callback ≈ $650 (ACCA); a card
that prevents two callbacks a year pays for a lot of software.

## 7. Companies are feedback loops; find the metric (Alexandr, Jeff)

A company is a feedback loop where humans run the edges. Agentic systems win
when the loop has a measurable objective and a fast evaluator.

**For Actober:** the loop is capture → review → publish → retrieve → apply →
outcome. The pilot dashboard already measures the front half. The back half
(retrieve → apply → outcome) is unmeasured today — and per these principles,
an unmeasured loop is an unimproved loop. Instrument application before
optimizing anything else.

## 8. Resilience is daily; earnestness beats sarcasm (Jensen, Sam)

"You don't have to overcome life in one day. Just get through today." And:
building real things is hard, taking shots is easy; hold the earnest bar.

**For Actober:** solo-founder company in an unglamorous trade. That is the
Scale-data position — unsexy is fine, compounding is the plan. Ship through
today, talk to the technician tomorrow, let the corpus grow.
